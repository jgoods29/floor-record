How we count

They work for you. Here's what they've been doing.

{{ site.site_name }} shows what members of the U.S. House did, with a link to the official record for every number. It does not grade anyone or tell you what to think.

Where the data comes from

Votes

Every roll call comes from the Office of the Clerk of the U.S. House. We download each official record and count from it directly. If our count ever differs from the Clerk's published total, the page says so.

Members

Names, districts and terms come from the public-domain congress-legislators project, maintained by volunteers and news organisations.

Bills and topics

Titles, sponsors, topics, official summaries, and the bills each member introduced or signed onto come from the Library of Congress through Congress.gov. Topic names are reworded for everyday readers.

What the numbers mean

Party position. On each vote, whichever of Yea or Nay most of a party's voting members chose. If a party split evenly, it had no position on that vote.

Party-split vote. A vote where most Republicans and most Democrats took opposite sides.

With party / against party. How often a member's Yea or Nay matched their own party's position. The headline figures and the leaderboard count only party-split votes, because near-unanimous votes make everyone look the same.

Broke with party. The member voted Yea or Nay and it differed from their party's position on that vote.

Attendance. The share of roll calls where a member voted Yea, Nay or Present. Members miss votes for many reasons, including illness and family emergencies; the record does not say why.

Left out. Quorum calls and the election of the Speaker are listed but not counted in any statistic. Members with fewer than {{ min_votes }} roll calls are not ranked. Delegates from D.C. and the territories can vote only on some amendments, so they have pages but are not ranked.

Plain-English wording

Names. Each vote is listed under the most recognisable name in the official record, such as "Retire through Ownership Act," with the bill number and formal wording in small print beneath it.

What a Yea or Nay meant. These lines come from fixed rules about House procedure. For example, a Yea on a motion to refer always means "send it to committee." They never guess at what a bill contains.

AI-written wording. Where a page says "written by AI," the short name or summary was drafted by an AI model using only official facts: the title, the Congressional Research Service summary where one exists, the kind of vote, and the tally. It is labelled, shown beside the official wording, and can contain mistakes.

The big votes. Votes featured at the top of the home page are recent votes that were close or where many members crossed party lines. Their headlines are either the automatic wording or wording a person has checked. Unreviewed AI text is never used there.

Routine procedural votes. Steps such as setting the terms of debate are part of the record and are counted in every statistic, but they are hidden from the front page and topic lists unless you ask to see them.

Words you will see

Bill. A proposed law. It must pass the House and the Senate and be signed by the President.

Resolution. A formal statement or decision by the House. Most resolutions do not become law.

Amendment. A proposed change to a bill.

Fast-track vote. A quicker process with no amendments, used for bills expected to be widely supported. It needs two-thirds of those voting. The official name is "suspension of the rules."

In committee. A bill has been filed and assigned to a smaller group of members to study. Most bills never leave this stage.

Signed onto. A member formally added their name in support of someone else's bill. The official word is "cosponsored."

Broke ranks. On a vote where the two parties took opposite sides, a member voted against most of their own party.

Free, and for the people

Free. Every page is open to everyone. There is no sign-up and nothing to buy.

No tracking. This site sets no cookies, runs no analytics and shows no ads. Its fonts and scripts are served from the site itself, so opening a page does not contact any other company. Like any website, the service that hosts these pages can see ordinary request logs; we do not use them.

No profit. Nobody is paid by the people or parties covered here, and nothing on the site is for sale.

Just knowledge. The site reports what members did, with a link to the official record for every number. It does not grade anyone or tell you how to vote.
